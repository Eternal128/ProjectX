Decomposing Language Model Progress into Memorization and Generalization

This is the technical research proposal, written for researchers. For a plain-language overview, start on the home page.

Project X 2026 (UofT AI), Research stream. Theme: What is Progress? Status: research proposal, 4 November 2026. Hypotheses are pre-registered (section 2); any later change is logged with a date and reason in prereg_changes.md.

Summary

Benchmark curves are how the field answers "what is progress?" for language models. Pythia 2.8B's accuracy on SciQ climbs from 21.9% to 82.9% in its first 13,000 training steps, about 9% of training, and adds only 5.3 more points over the remaining 91% (EleutherAI's published results). But a score rises for two reasons it cannot tell apart: the model may have become more capable, or it may have seen the test items during training and recalled them. Because Pythia's training data and its order are public, we can date the moment each benchmark question was first read and measure how much of the progress curve that reading explains.

Contributions:

  • Exposure dating. Find benchmark items in the Pile and date their first exposure to the training step, with hand-validated precision per tier (sections 5.1 to 5.3).
  • A causal effect of exposure. An event study around each item's first exposure, with a pre-trend test (section 5.6).
  • The share of progress that is exposure. An exposure-aware IRT model and its counterfactual, tested for dose and ability effects and transfer to paraphrases (sections 5.5, 5.7, 5.8).

Pythia is unusually well suited to this. Every model in the suite was trained on the same public corpus (the Pile) in the same fixed, published order, with 154 checkpoints saved along the way. That lets us find benchmark items in the training data, name the training step at which each one was first seen, and watch the model's performance on that item at checkpoints before and after.

We combine two tools. Exposure detection finds benchmark items in the Pile and maps each match to the step where Pythia first trained on it. An exposure IRT model, checked by a model-free difference-in-differences event study, then estimates how much easier an item becomes after exposure and what share of measured progress that accounts for.

The closest prior work is Lesci et al. (2024), who run difference-in-differences on Pythia for training sequences. We study benchmark items instead, their effect on measured progress, and add the exposure IRT model.

1. Motivation

Benchmark curves over training are the standard picture of "progress". If part of a curve's rise is recall of items that happened to appear in training data, the curve overstates capability, and the overstatement can grow or shrink as training proceeds. Contamination studies usually ask a static question (is this item in the training data?). We ask a dynamic one: when the model sees an item, does its performance on that item jump, and how much of the curve do such jumps add up to?

Answering it requires three things that rarely come together: the training data, its exact order, and many intermediate checkpoints. Pythia provides all three.

2. Research questions and pre-registered hypotheses

# Question Hypothesis
RQ1 Does IRT ability tell a different story of progress than raw accuracy across Pythia's training? H1. Accuracy and ability disagree on when progress speeds up or stalls.
RQ2 Does seeing an item in training cause a jump in performance on it? H2. Log-probability of the correct answer rises sharply right after exposure, relative to matched clean items, with no pre-trend.
RQ3 How much of measured progress is explained by exposure? H3. The exposure term is positive and grows with copy count within standard Pythia.
RQ4 Does the exposure effect depend on ability? H4. The effect changes with ability, so memorization and generalization do not simply add up.
RQ5 Do exposure gains transfer to paraphrased items? H5. Gains transfer only partly to paraphrases.

Every hypothesis is a valid result if it fails. Thresholds are never tuned, and items are never dropped, to make a hypothesis pass.

  • Lesci et al. (2024), Causal Estimation of Memorisation Profiles (ACL). Difference-in-differences on Pythia checkpoints to estimate how much a model memorises the sequences it trains on. Our design borrows their causal framing; we differ in studying benchmark items rather than training sequences, in measuring the effect on benchmark progress, and in the exposure IRT model.
  • Biderman et al. (2023), Pythia. The model suite, checkpoints and reproducible data order we rely on.
  • Biderman et al. (2023); Carlini et al. (2023). Memorization in language models: what predicts it and how it scales.
  • Liu et al. (2024), Infini-gram. Exact n-gram search over trillion-token corpora, used for candidate retrieval in the Pile.
  • Polo et al. (2024), tinyBenchmarks. IRT applied to language-model benchmarks.
  • Schaeffer et al. (2023). Apparent "emergence" can be an artefact of the metric, one reason we model continuous log-probability as well as binary accuracy.

Only Lesci et al. has been checked against the source so far; the other references are to be verified before any citation in a write-up.

4. Setting

4.1 Models

Pythia 410M, 1.4B and 2.8B, each in two versions: trained on the standard Pile and on the deduplicated Pile. Checkpoints load from Hugging Face with revision="stepN".

From the Pythia repository README (checked 4 November 2026):

  • All model sizes are trained on the exact same data in the exact same order.
  • Each model saw 299,892,736,000 tokens: 143,000 steps at a batch of 2,097,152 tokens, that is 1,024 sequences of 2,048 tokens (stored as 2,049-token rows so targets can be shifted).
  • The standard models see just under one epoch of the Pile; the deduplicated models see about 1.5 epochs of the 207B-token deduplicated Pile.
  • 154 checkpoints per model: steps 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, …, 512, 1000, and every 1,000 steps after that.
  • The preshuffled, pre-tokenized training data is public (EleutherAI/pile-standard-pythia-preshuffled and EleutherAI/pile-deduped-pythia-preshuffled), with a batch viewer that reads rows step × 1024 to (step + 1) × 1024 for a given training step.

4.2 Checkpoints

We evaluate 30 checkpoints per model, denser early in training, all inside the documented schedule: 512, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 6000, 8000, 10000, 13000, 16000, 20000, 24000, 28000, 33000, 38000, 43000, 49000, 55000, 61000, 68000, 75000, 82000, 90000, 98000, 106000, 114000, 122000, 130000, 137000 and 143000 (configs/models.yaml). Before the main run we confirm each revision exists on the Hugging Face hub (python -m evals.list_checkpoints).

4.3 Benchmarks

Each benchmark must have been public before the Pile was released (end of 2020), and Pythia must score clearly above chance on it: at least 5 points above chance with a one-sided test against chance at p < 0.01 (configs/benchmarks.yaml).

Candidates: TriviaQA (2017), SQuAD (2016), LAMBADA (2016), ARC-Easy (2018), SciQ (2017), PIQA (2019), with years from each paper's first arXiv version. Excluded on purpose: GSM8K (released 2021, so it cannot be in the Pile) and MMLU (near chance at this scale; see below).

Above-chance check from published results. EleutherAI publishes zero-shot accuracy for every Pythia model at 27 checkpoints (lm-evaluation-harness, Apache-2.0, commit a19eecb). They cover four of our candidates and MMLU. Final-checkpoint accuracy:

5. Method

5.1 Exposure detection

  1. Candidate retrieval. Query infini-gram over the Pile with long n-grams (13 or more tokens) from each question and each answer, plus one span that joins the end of the question to the answer, and shorter n-grams for a wider candidate set.
  2. Semantic check. Embed each candidate passage and the item with an open sentence-embedding model and store their cosine similarity.
  3. Tiers. confirmed: a long exact match covering both question and answer. likely: high semantic similarity with no exact match. clean: no candidate above a low similarity threshold. Items in between are ambiguous and left out of both groups.
  4. Hand validation. Two people label a random sample of about 100 items per tier; we report precision per tier.

Missed exposure puts seen items in the clean group, which biases every effect toward zero. We state this wherever results are reported.

5.2 Step mapping

Infini-gram returns positions in the Pile, not training steps. Training step b consumes rows 1024·b to 1024·(b+1) − 1 of the preshuffled data, so a matched token span maps to the batch that contains it. Checkpoint stepN has seen batches 0 to N − 1, so we define the first-exposure step as t0 = first batch + 1, the exposure indicator as E(t) = 1 when t ≥ t0, and the running copy count n(t) as the number of occurrences in batches before t. The off-by-one is confirmed against the batch viewer before the first real run.

Two ways to locate matches in training order are compared in week 1: (a) search the preshuffled token files directly with a multi-pattern matcher, which also yields copy counts; or (b) map Pile document indices to the shuffled order, if the tooling allows. The choice and its storage and time cost are recorded in step_mapping.md.

5.3 Timing filter

A confirmed item enters the event study only if at least three evaluated checkpoints come before its t0 and t0 falls between 10% and 90% of training. All results are reported stratified by t0.

5.4 Checkpoint evaluation

EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness with per-sample logging. For every model, checkpoint and item we store binary correctness y and the log-probability of the correct answer logp, in one table with columns model, data_version, step, benchmark, item_id, y, logp, tier, t0, n. Seeds and library versions are pinned. The inputs are never mixed: the IRT model uses y only; the event study uses logp only.

5.5 Exposure IRT

For item i, model m and checkpoint t:

P(y_imt = 1) = sigmoid( a_i · (θ_mt − b_i + δ_imt · E_imt) )
δ_imt        = δ_0 + δ_1 · log(1 + n_it) + δ_2 · θ_mt

θ_mt is the ability of one model at one checkpoint, with a Normal(0, 1) prior that fixes the scale; b_i is base difficulty; a_i > 0 is discrimination; δ_1 is the dose term (copy count) and δ_2 the ability interaction, the first term dropped if anything fails to fit. A standard 2PL model without the exposure term is fitted for comparison (RQ1).

Fitting proceeds one step at a time, each gated on the previous: (1) MAP with a single shared δ on simulated data, with a parameter-recovery test; (2) dose and ability terms added, recovery tested again; (3) stochastic variational inference on real data for approximate uncertainty; (4) full NUTS on a random subset of items, only to confirm the variational intervals.

5.6 Event study

Each confirmed item is matched to clean items from the same benchmark with similar base difficulty, measured as mean logp over checkpoints in the first 10% of training (before any eligible t0). With event time k counted in checkpoints from exposure:

Effect(k) = [mean_exp(t0+k) − mean_exp(t0−1)] − [mean_clean(t0+k) − mean_clean(t0−1)]

with 95% confidence intervals from a bootstrap over items. Pre-trend test: if exposed items were already rising faster than their matches before t0 (the bootstrap interval of the mean pre-period effect excludes zero), that group's estimate is not read causally, and we say so.

5.7 Exposure share of progress

From the fitted IRT model, the counterfactual accuracy with every E set to zero gives

M = (ΔAcc_observed − ΔAcc_E0) / ΔAcc_observed

the share of the observed accuracy gain explained by exposure (RQ3).

5.8 Robustness

  • Add the likely tier to each side (exposed, then clean) in turn.
  • Transfer (RQ5): paraphrase a sample of confirmed items with new wording, names and numbers, and compare their gains with the originals'.
  • Dose: the main dose measure is copy count within standard Pythia. The deduplicated run is a secondary check only, because deduplication changed the whole data distribution, not just the copies.

6. Preliminary work

Code. The repository contains the full pipeline: exposure detection and step mapping, the evaluation wrapper and table schema, the IRT models in NumPyro, the event study, and one script per figure. Every run is driven by a config file and records its config hash and git commit. 27 unit tests pass, including the IRT recovery tests required by fitting steps 1 and 2.

Benchmark curves. The published results above also give accuracy over training at 27 checkpoints for each benchmark and model. They rise steadily for all four candidate benchmarks and stay flat near chance for MMLU. We use them only to describe the benchmarks; no threshold or hypothesis depends on them.

Method checks on simulated data. Our own code, run on simulated data with known effects (python -m figures.site_methods):

  • Event study. 120 exposed and 360 clean simulated items on the real 30-checkpoint schedule, with a planted jump of 1.5 in log-probability at exposure and no pre-trend. The estimated effect after exposure is between 1.41 and 1.50 at every event time from 0 to 8; before exposure it stays within ±0.06 of zero, and the pre-trend test does not fire.
  • Pre-trend detection. The same panel with a planted pre-exposure rise: the test flags it (mean pre-period effect −0.62, 95% interval −0.68 to −0.56).
  • Exposure IRT. 200 simulated items, two models and 20 checkpoints with a planted δ_0 = 1.0: MAP recovers δ_0 = 0.985, with correlations of 0.91 for item difficulty and 0.97 for ability.

These show the estimators recover what is planted. They say nothing yet about Pythia.

7. Threats to validity

  • Missed exposure (paraphrased, reformatted or translated copies) puts seen items in the clean group and biases effects toward zero. Hand validation of the clean tier bounds this.
  • False matches in the confirmed tier (generic questions with common answers) dilute the effect. The confirmed tier requires question and answer together; hand validation reports its precision.
  • Selection by timing. The timing filter keeps items first seen mid-training, which may differ from items seen early or late. Results are stratified by t0.
  • Matching. Matched clean items may differ from exposed ones in ways difficulty does not capture; the pre-trend test is the main guard.
  • Harness details. Prompt format and scoring affect accuracy. We pin versions, and use log-probability as well as accuracy so that conclusions do not rest on a threshold metric.
  • Deduplication changes the whole training distribution, so standard-versus-deduplicated comparisons are secondary evidence only.

8. Go / no-go gate

The main analysis needs at least 200 confirmed exposed items that pass the timing filter. If fewer, the likely tier moves into the main analysis and the ability interaction δ_2 is dropped. The count and decision are recorded in prereg_changes.md at the end of week 1.
